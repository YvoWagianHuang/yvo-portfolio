I have been busy designing a **cross-border collaborative online course** over the past two weeks, which came to a successful conclusion yesterday. It's time to reflect and share some thoughts.

**First, a brief overview of the course background**: The instructors were professors in the field of international cultures from National Taiwan Normal University (NTNU) and a Thai university. The students were undergraduates from both universities. The course was taught entirely in English and conducted via **Google Meet** over two weeks. The goal of the course was for students from both universities to exchange ideas and gain deeper insights and discussion on **Inter-Asia popular culture**.

> The first week's class did not go smoothly due to technical issues that interrupted the session for nearly half an hour. Moreover, most students were unclear about their tasks and failed to achieve effective learning. After the session ended, both the professors and I felt that we had ruined it.

## Starting the Design Process

I was the teaching assistant (TA) on the NTNU side. After obtaining permission from the instructor, Eva, she gave me full authority to redesign the course for the second week. I immediately addressed the shortcomings of the first week and designed about 80% of the course outline for the second week. Here is the breakdown of my thought process:

### First, we must think about the relationship between the students and the learning objectives:

1. **Students' starting point**: They didn't understand the value of the course or the significance of popular culture.
2. **End point of learning (objectives)**: What the instructors hoped students would achieve (communication on popular culture), which could be presented through a presentation or an assignment.
3. **Course design**: This is the path that connects the students' starting point to their destination.

### Now, we can consider what blocked students from reaching the destination, and how to solve it.

The first step was to analyze specific **friction points** from student feedback. We asked students to fill out feedback forms after both sessions. In the first week, some students provided insightful feedback worth reflecting on:

> 1. *When we're assigned to small groups, we can no longer hear what's going on in the main group, and no one was talking or responding in chat for quite some time, so that was a bit confusing at first.*
> 2. *Wish everything will go smoothly next time.*
> 3. *Some people might be shy or have some technical problems, so it's kinda embarrassed to discuss.*
> 4. *Thank you for organizing this session. It was a great opportunity to exchange ideas and learn about Taiwanese culture and perspectives from students in Taiwan.*

From the students' feedback, I categorized the **obstacles and their underlying causes**, and designed corresponding **solutions**:

### 🎯 Student Obstacles and Week 2 Solutions

*   **Didn't know what to do**
    *   *Possible causes*: Unclear guidance and ambiguous learning path.
    *   *Week 1 approach*: No slides provided; went directly into the session.
    *   *Week 2 solution*: **Create slides to explain today's agenda first**, and repeatedly post announcements in the Google Meet chat.
*   **Low learning motivation**
    *   *Possible causes*: Felt the course purpose was unclear and didn't understand its value.
    *   *Week 1 approach*: No explanation provided; went directly into the session.
    *   *Week 2 solution*: **Create slides to first explain the significance and value of the course**.
*   **Inability to discuss effectively**
    *   *Possible causes*: Some students were hesitant to speak English and could only rely on typing in the chat room, making it difficult to preserve productive outputs.
    *   *Week 1 approach*: Instructors joined specific groups to guide discussions.
    *   *Week 2 solution*: **Pre-fill a shared Google Slide template** with detailed guiding questions and reference materials. Guide students to complete a **co-edited presentation** for in-depth discussion on the topic.
*   **Loose course pacing**
    *   *Possible causes*: Unfamiliarity with Google Meet features, encountering many technical issues.
    *   *Week 1 approach*: Class was interrupted to troubleshoot technical issues, which couldn't be resolved, leading to chaos in breakout groups.
    *   *Week 2 solution*: **Abandon unresolvable breakout technical issues**, start with random assignment, then manually adjust certain group members, and design a much more detailed lesson flow in advance.

### ⏱️ Week 2 Course Structure

Based on these reflections, I designed the following course structure for Week 2:

1.  **Background Information Introduction (30 mins)**
    *   *Content*: The instructor reviewed the previous week's lesson, explained today's agenda, the collaborative background of the course, evaluation methods, and introductory readings for each topic.
    *   *Design Motivation*: Resolve the issue of unclear learning paths for students and reduce extraneous cognitive load.
    *   *Actual Results*: **Successful**. Most students clearly understood their learning tasks, allowing the session to proceed smoothly.
2.  **Group Hands-on Activity (50 mins)**
    *   *Content*: Students were randomly divided into nine groups to complete presentations on assigned topics based on the slide outlines and prompts.
    *   *Design Motivation*: Provide a collaborative platform to facilitate discussion, visualize outcomes, support the upcoming shareback, and promote intra-group peer learning.
    *   *Actual Results*: All nine groups completed their presentations. **The step-by-step guiding questions served as scaffolding**, giving depth to the presentation content.
3.  **Student Shareback & Feedback (20 mins)**
    *   *Content*: Students volunteered or were designated to present their slides, followed by instructor feedback and questions.
    *   *Design Motivation*: Enable inter-group learning, allowing groups working on the same topic to observe others' angles, and helping the whole class understand all topics.
    *   *Actual Results*: **A total of four groups participated in the shareback presentation**. All were able to discuss their topics thoroughly, and some students supplemented information in the chat.
4.  **Instructor Wrap-up & Q&A (20 mins)**
    *   *Content*: The instructor summarized student sharebacks and opened the floor for Q&A among students.
    *   *Design Motivation*: Promote interaction between students from both universities and summarize course content.
    *   *Actual Results*: A total of three students proactively asked questions.

## The Core of Scaffolding: Design Rationale for Co-Edited Google Slides

In this course, I consider the most successful design to be the **co-edited Google Slides**.

By designing a **slide template**, instructors from both universities could fill in information and complete instructional materials based on the embedded prompts. During the presentation phase, students could share their slides to express their ideas more clearly.

We designed the following task and responsibility structure based on the slide outline:

*   **Title Slide**
    *   *Design Motivation*: Cover page.
    *   *Learning Task*: Write down team members and group number.
    *   *Instructor Task*: N/A.
*   **Self-Introduction**
    *   *Design Motivation*: Icebreaker activity, select team leader and note-taker.
    *   *Learning Task*: 30-second self-introduction and select roles.
    *   *Instructor Task*: Provide external motivation for students (e.g., bonus points).
*   **Guiding Questions**
    *   *Design Motivation*: Help students discuss topics progressively from shallow to deep.
    *   *Learning Task*: Read and discuss the questions.
    *   *Instructor Task*: **Design questions across three levels** (1. Define and observe phenomena, 2. Attribution and analysis, 3. Conclusion and inference).
*   **References**
    *   *Design Motivation*: Help students focus on the direction instructors intended for discussion, providing meaningful texts to support learning.
    *   *Learning Task*: Read texts and discover the connections between the texts and the guiding questions.
    *   *Instructor Task*: Provide professional texts related to the topic.
*   **Discussion Area**
    *   *Design Motivation*: Allow students to post their opinions and cited materials here.
    *   *Learning Task*: Engage in discussion based on the guiding questions.
    *   *Instructor Task*: N/A.
*   **Conclusion Area**
    *   *Design Motivation*: Allow students to present their discussion results.
    *   *Learning Task*: Write conclusions in English.
    *   *Instructor Task*: N/A.
*   **English Writing Structure Guidance**
    *   *Design Motivation*: Provide a basic English writing framework for students from both universities to reduce anxiety and improve writing quality.
    *   *Learning Task*: Discuss using the English writing framework as a reference.
    *   *Instructor Task*: N/A.

## Looking Ahead: Where Can We Do Better?

Some students provided further suggestions in the Week 2 feedback form. Below are my initial thoughts and future directions for improvement:

1.  **Student Dynamics in Grouping**
    *   *Feedback*: *"There are some friends who are so outspoken that it leaves me speechless, making it difficult to continue the conversation."*
    *   *Cause Analysis*: Random grouping cannot guarantee good team chemistry in every group.
    *   *Solution*: **Design some individual assignments** so that more introverted students can also express their ideas fully.
2.  **Loss of Cross-Border Team Members**
    *   *Feedback*: *"Really appreciate the collaboration, but there was no Thai student in our team... There was actually one and then gone"*
    *   *Cause Analysis*: After random grouping, the ratio of Thai to Taiwanese students wasn't adjusted in time. Although noticed at the moment, the possibility of students dropping offline directly wasn't anticipated.
    *   *Solution*: **Use Zoom software**. Zoom's Breakout Room feature is more mature in management and stability.
3.  **Insufficient Icebreaker Activity**
    *   *Feedback*: *"I hope next time if we have something similar, let's have the icebreaking section first!"*
    *   *Cause Analysis*: No icebreaker activity was introduced in Week 1. Although Week 2 slides intentionally included a self-introduction section, the effect still seemed insufficient.
    *   *Solution*: **Introduce icebreaker activities starting in Week 1**, though this requires stronger technical support and prior guidance.
4.  **Insufficient Reading and Discussion Time**
    *   *Feedback*: *"From my perspective need more conversion time"*
    *   *Cause Analysis*: Although 50 minutes of discussion time were allocated in Week 2, the heavy reading load from references left some students feeling overwhelmed.
    *   *Solution*: **Allocate more study hours** or appropriately reduce the reading load during class.

## Reflection and Final Thoughts

For me personally, the biggest takeaway from designing this course was seeing satisfied smiles on the faces of professors from both universities.

> Online teaching is extremely difficult and requires meticulously designed courses.

In the first week, I could clearly feel the anxiety of the instructors and the impatience and confusion of the students. However, the redesigned course in the second week ran smoothly and effectively, significantly enhancing the students' learning experience.

My philosophy is: **Make everyone fall in love with learning**.

This was my first time designing a large-scale international online course in English, as well as a major breakthrough in my personal career. I am thrilled to have had the valuable opportunity to take on this challenge.
